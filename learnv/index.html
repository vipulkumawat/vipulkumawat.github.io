Chapter 01 · Foundations

What Is Meaning, To a Machine?

A computer has never smelled a dog, chased a bus, or felt monsoon rain. It only has numbers. So when we ask a machine "is puppy similar to dog?", we're asking something it has no native way to answer — unless we first turn words into numbers in a way that preserves closeness.

That's the entire premise of this academy. Everything from here on — vectors, distance, embeddings, RAG — is one long answer to a single question: how do you place meaning at a coordinate?

The library analogy A well-run library doesn't shelve books alphabetically by title — it shelves them by subject, using a system like Dewey Decimal. A book on Mughal architecture sits near one on Rajasthani forts, not because their titles match, but because their meaning does. An embedding is just a Dewey Decimal number with hundreds of digits instead of one — precise enough that "similar meaning" becomes "nearby shelf."

Chapter 02 · Foundations

Vectors: An Instruction, Not a Dot

A vector isn't really a point — it's a set of directions from somewhere. "3 units right, 2 units up" is a vector whether it starts at your house or the origin. What matters is the displacement: how far, and in which direction.

Every embedding you'll ever use is exactly this, just in far more than two dimensions — a list of numbers, each one a direction to move along some invisible axis of meaning.

Google Maps analogy When Maps says "head 400m northeast," it never tells you the destination's absolute coordinates — just the displacement from where you're standing. A word vector works the same way: not an absolute "meaning-coordinate" you'd recognize, but an instruction for how far to walk, and in which direction, from a neutral starting point.

Chapter 03 · Foundations

Distance: How Far Apart Are Two Meanings?

Once meaning lives at a coordinate, "similar" becomes "nearby." The most obvious way to measure "nearby" is the one you already know from school: the straight-line distance between two points.

The autorickshaw-meter analogy An auto meter doesn't care about the maze of one-way streets it actually drove — some apps just estimate "as the crow flies" distance for a fare preview. Euclidean distance is exactly that crow-flies line between two embeddings: it ignores the "roads" of the space and just measures the straight gap.

Under the hood
d(A,B) = √Σ(aᵢ − bᵢ)²
def euclidean(a: list[float], b: list[float]) -> float:
    return sum((x - y) ** 2 for x, y in zip(a, b)) ** 0.5

Time complexity is O(d) per comparison, d = dimensions — cheap for one pair, expensive across millions. That single fact is why vector databases exist: to avoid comparing your query against every row (Chapters 7–8).

Chapter 04 · Foundations

Dot Product: Are You Pulling the Same Way?

Distance asks "how far apart." The dot product asks a different question entirely: how much do these two vectors point the same way? Multiply matching components and add them up — a number that grows when vectors agree in direction, shrinks or goes negative when they don't.

The tug-of-war analogy Two people pulling a cart: if they pull in exactly the same direction, their effort adds up fully — maximum dot product. Pull at right angles and half the effort is wasted sideways — dot product near zero. Pull against each other and you're fighting your own teammate — a negative dot product.

Chapter 05 · Foundations

Cosine Similarity: The Workhorse Metric

Take the dot product, then divide out the magnitudes — and suddenly you no longer care how long either vector is, only which direction it points. That single normalization is why cosine similarity, not raw distance, is the default metric in almost every production vector database.

The WhatsApp chat-search analogy Search your chat history for "where's my order" and you'd want it to surface a two-word reply and a five-paragraph rant equally well, as long as both are about the same thing. Cosine similarity ignores message length (magnitude) entirely and compares only topic (direction) — which is exactly the behavior you want from semantic search.

Under the hood
cos θ = (A·B) / (‖A‖‖B‖)
def cosine_similarity(a: list[float], b: list[float]) -> float:
    dot = sum(x * y for x, y in zip(a, b))
    mag_a = sum(x * x for x in a) ** 0.5
    mag_b = sum(y * y for y in b) ** 0.5
    return dot / (mag_a * mag_b)
// Java
static double cosineSimilarity(double[] a, double[] b) {
    double dot = 0, magA = 0, magB = 0;
    for (int i = 0; i < a.length; i++) {
        dot += a[i] * b[i];
        magA += a[i] * a[i];
        magB += b[i] * b[i];
    }
    return dot / (Math.sqrt(magA) * Math.sqrt(magB));
}

Chapter 06 · Foundations

The Curse of Dimensionality

Everything so far has quietly relied on one assumption: that "nearest" means something. In 2D, it obviously does — your eyes just confirmed it four chapters in a row. But real embeddings live in 384 to 3072 dimensions, and something strange happens out there: as dimensions grow, every point drifts toward being roughly equidistant from every other point. Nearest and farthest stop being meaningfully different.

The rush-hour local-train analogy In an empty train compartment, "who's standing closest to the door" is obvious. Pack that same compartment to Mumbai-local rush-hour density and the question stops making sense — everyone is closest to everyone, personal space has collapsed uniformly. High-dimensional space does this to points automatically, without needing rush hour to fill it — dimensions alone are enough.

This is a real, well-known result (Beyer et al., "When Is Nearest Neighbor Meaningful?", 1999) — not an artifact of this demo. It's exactly why production embedding models cap out around a few hundred to a few thousand dimensions instead of using ten thousand "to be safe": past a point, extra dimensions add cost without adding discriminative power, and can actively hurt exact-distance search. It's also a big part of why cosine similarity (angle) is preferred over Euclidean distance (magnitude) at high dimensionality — and why approximate search (Chapter 11) isn't a shortcut so much as an acknowledgment that exact "nearest" was never that meaningful to begin with, out there.

Chapter 07 · Foundations

Tokenization: What Actually Goes In

One thing got glossed over since Chapter 1: an embedding model never sees your raw sentence. Text is first broken into tokens — word pieces from a fixed vocabulary — and each token is mapped to an integer ID. Those IDs are what actually get embedded. This single fact quietly explains LLM API pricing (billed per token, not per word), context-window limits (measured in tokens), and why "count the characters" is the wrong way to estimate either.

The courier-parcel-box analogy A courier company doesn't stock a custom box for every possible item — it has a fixed set of standard box sizes and fits your oddly-shaped parcel using a combination of them. A tokenizer works the same way: it has a fixed vocabulary of ~50,000–100,000 "boxes" (tokens), and any text you throw at it — including words it has never seen — gets packed into a sequence of those standard pieces.

This demo uses a small hand-built vocabulary and greedy longest-match splitting — enough to show the idea, not the real algorithm. Production tokenizers (OpenAI's tiktoken, BERT's WordPiece, Llama/T5's SentencePiece) learn their vocabulary from a giant corpus using Byte-Pair Encoding: start with individual characters, then repeatedly merge whichever adjacent pair occurs most often across the training data, until the vocabulary hits its target size (often 50k–100k tokens).

def bpe_train_step(corpus_pairs: dict[tuple, int], vocab: set) -> tuple:
    most_common = max(corpus_pairs, key=corpus_pairs.get)
    vocab.add(''.join(most_common))   # merge the pair into one new token
    return most_common

Because that training corpus is overwhelmingly English, common English words often become a single token while the same sentence in Hindi, Tamil, or Marathi frequently costs more tokens per character — a real, measurable tax that shows up directly in API bills and context-window budgets for Indic-language products.

Chapter 08 · From Math to Meaning

Embeddings: A Whole City Block of Meaning

Zoom out from two vectors to thousands, and something remarkable happens: words that mean similar things cluster together, entirely unsupervised. Nobody told the model "fruits go here" — it discovered that geometry from reading text.

The PIN-code analogy Two neighbourhoods can have wildly different postal PIN codes yet feel culturally identical — same language, same street food, same festival calendar. An embedding space is a PIN-code system redesigned from scratch so that codes are close exactly when the culture is close. That's what "training an embedding model" means: relabeling the world by similarity, not by geography.

Chapter 09 · From Math to Meaning

How Embedding Models Actually Learn

Nobody sat down and hand-labeled "mango is 0.82 similar to banana." Embedding models learn geometry from pairs: millions of examples of "these two things belong together" (a question and its answer, a sentence and its paraphrase, a product and its review) and, just as importantly, examples of things that don't. Training just nudges vectors — pull positives together, push negatives apart — repeated billions of times until the geometry settles into something useful.

The marriage-bureau analogy A matchmaker never asks you to define "compatibility" as a formula. They just observe outcomes — this pairing worked, that one didn't — across thousands of introductions, and slowly build an instinct for which traits pull people together and which push them apart. Contrastive learning is that instinct, formalized: no one defines "similar meaning" either — the model just gets shown enough positive and negative pairs to work out the geometry itself.

Three design choices matter once you're picking a model, not just using one:

Dense · Sparse · Multi-vector

Dense (what this whole site assumes): one fixed-length vector per text, every dimension populated. Good general-purpose default.

Sparse (e.g. SPLADE): mostly-zero vectors where each dimension maps to an actual vocabulary term — less "black box," great at exact terms (part numbers, names).

Multi-vector (e.g. ColBERT): one small vector per token, compared via "late interaction." More accurate, far more storage and compute.

Matryoshka Representation Learning

Newer models (OpenAI's text-embedding-3, Nomic Embed) are trained so the first 256 dimensions of a 1536-dim vector are already a decent standalone embedding. You can truncate for a cheaper, smaller index and lose only a little accuracy — instead of the usual all-or-nothing tradeoff.

Chapter 10 · Systems

The Vector Database Itself

A vector database is boring on purpose: an embedding model turns content into vectors, an index organizes those vectors for fast search, and storage keeps everything durable, filterable, and queryable at scale. The magic isn't storage — it's the index (Chapter 8).

The warehouse analogy A giant Amazon fulfillment warehouse doesn't just pile boxes randomly — items are shelved by a system so a picker's route to any item is short. A vector database is that warehouse for meaning: the embedding model labels each box, the index decides which shelf, and search is just a very well-planned walk to the nearest shelf.

Chapter 11 · Systems

ANN Search: Why HNSW Feels Like Cheating

Comparing your query against every stored vector is exact but doesn't scale — O(n) per query against a billion rows is a non-starter. HNSW (Hierarchical Navigable Small World) trades a sliver of accuracy for enormous speed by building a multi-layer graph and searching greedily, top layer down.

The highway-system analogy To drive across India you don't take local roads the whole way — you take a National Highway to the right region, a State Highway to the right district, then local roads for the last kilometre. HNSW's top layer is sparse, like NH express lanes connecting a few hub cities; each layer down adds more, smaller roads, until layer 0 has every street. Search enters at the top and only "gets local" once it's already close.

Chapter 12 · Systems

Beyond HNSW: Index Types & Quantization

HNSW isn't the only index, and index choice is only half the budget — quantization, how precisely each number is stored, is the other half. The two are independent knobs: pick an index for search speed, pick a quantization level for memory, and combine them.

Continuing the warehouse-and-highway analogy If HNSW (Chapter 11) is the highway system connecting shelves, quantization is repacking the boxes themselves — same items, tighter packaging, so more fit on the same truck. You lose a little labeling precision on the box, but you can now ship four times as much on one trip.

Scalar quantization (int8) maps each float32 to one byte — ~4× smaller, negligible accuracy loss. Product quantization (PQ) splits each vector into subvectors, clusters each subspace independently, and stores only cluster IDs — 16–64× smaller, some accuracy cost, the workhorse of billion-scale search (Faiss's IVF-PQ). Binary quantization keeps 1 bit per dimension and compares with Hamming distance — extremely fast and small, usually paired with a rescoring pass over the top candidates using the original full-precision vectors to recover accuracy.

Chapter 13 · Applied

The Retrieval Pipeline: Search Doesn't End at "Nearest"

Production search is never just "find the closest vector." Real systems chunk documents sensibly, filter by metadata before or alongside the vector search, blend vector similarity with old-fashioned keyword matching, and often rerank the shortlist with a slower, smarter model.

The e-commerce-search analogy Search "waterproof shoes" on any shopping app and four things happen at once: your query is matched semantically, results are filtered by what's actually in stock and ships to your pincode, exact-keyword matches for "waterproof" get a boost, and the final order is reshuffled by a ranking model tuned on what people actually click. That's this entire chapter, in one search bar.
Chapter 14 · Applied

When "Nearest" Isn't Enough: Query Transformation

Sometimes the query and the answer just don't share vocabulary — "how do I get my money back" and "Section 4: Refund Eligibility" can be conceptually identical and still sit further apart in embedding space than you'd like. Advanced retrieval rewrites the query before searching, instead of hoping embeddings alone will bridge the gap.

Remember the librarian from Chapter 1? Walk up to a good librarian and ask "that book about the guy who did the thing," and they don't search literally — they guess what you probably mean and search for that. HyDE makes the retrieval system do exactly this: draft a plausible answer first, then search using the draft instead of the vague question.

Chapter 16 · Capstone

Building RAG: The Whole Academy, One Query

Retrieval-Augmented Generation is every idea in this academy chained together: embed the question, search the index, retrieve and rerank chunks, then hand the LLM exactly the material it needs to answer honestly instead of guessing.

The open-book-exam analogy A closed-book exam forces you to answer from memory alone — confident, sometimes wrong. RAG turns the LLM's exam open-book: before answering, it's handed the specific pages (chunks) most relevant to the question, and grading rewards citing them. That's the entire reason RAG reduces hallucination — it's not a smarter model, it's a better-prepared one.
